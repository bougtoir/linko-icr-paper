We submit our manuscript, "The Information Contribution Ratio: a reproducible descriptor of endpoint informational weight in meta-analysis, and what it does and does not diagnose", for consideration as a Research Article in *Statistics in Medicine*.

### What the paper does

Meta-analysis reduces each trial to one effect estimate for one endpoint, while trials differ substantially in how many variables they measure and in how the endpoint sits within that variable space. We formalise this structural feature as the Information Contribution Ratio (ICR), give an explicit estimand, and provide estimators computable from published baseline tables and from individual participant data. We then test, rather than assume, the natural hypothesis that dispersion in ICR is associated with statistical heterogeneity.

### Why we think it suits the journal

The contribution is methodological and largely negative, which we believe is of value to the meta-analysis literature. In a simulation study reported according to the ADEMP structure and with Monte Carlo standard errors throughout, the heterogeneous-ICR scenario did not show higher I-squared than the uniform-ICR scenario (difference -0.41 percentage points, 95% Monte Carlo confidence interval [-1.91, 1.10]), and a negative control in which ICR varied with no structural mechanism produced comparable heterogeneity (mean I-squared 11.0%). A redundancy analysis shows that the dimension-counting estimator changes to 0.50 times its value when near-duplicate covariates are added, while an eigenvalue-based effective-dimension version is essentially unchanged (ratio 1.01).

We therefore present ICR as a reproducible structural descriptor and reporting adjunct rather than as a diagnostic for whether pooling is appropriate, and we state the limits of the measure explicitly. An exploratory analysis of 8 country groups of the International Stroke Trial (open individual participant data) shows the principal-component estimators are computable and vary meaningfully; the regression-based estimator was associated with 14-day mortality (P = 0.003). Because those groups are recruitment strata of one trial and not independent studies, we report this as hypothesis-generating only.

### Reproducibility

Every number, table and figure in the manuscript is regenerated by a single command from public inputs: the International Stroke Trial data are downloaded by a script from the University of Edinburgh DataShare repository, and the published trial inputs are stored as comma-separated files with per-study citations. The manuscript is built by reading the generated result files, so no value is transcribed by hand; an audit script in the repository verifies this before each build. The analysis reported here was produced with Python 3.10.12, NumPy 2.2.6 and pandas 2.3.3 at commit c27eb0d6.
